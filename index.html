Part 1 · 3 min

What is statistical learning?

Statistical learning means using data to guess something you cannot see directly, and also saying how sure you are about that guess.

Gaming versions of what your course does: Will our team win this round? Is this player cheating? Which hero will the enemy pick next? What is this player's real headshot rate, not just today's lucky number?

Your course does this the Bayesian way. You start with a belief. You see some data. You update the belief. You already do this in games: a new player joins your team, you have no idea how good they are, then after three rounds of whiffing you change your mind. Coins, dice, spam filters, Gaussians and regression in the course all repeat this one move.

The first lecture used a game with a globe. We will use a CS2 version: every kill is either a Headshot or a Body shot. From a few kills, guess the player's true headshot rate. We play it ourselves in Part 7.

Before the data

Prior

What you believe before looking. “Most players hit about half their kills as headshots.”

From the data

Likelihood

How well each possible answer explains what you saw. “If their rate were 90%, three body shots in a row would be strange.”

After the data

Posterior

Your updated belief about the player. Prior and likelihood combined.

Part 2 · 4 min

Probability is a number for “how sure”

Three basic rules

  • A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • All possible outcomes together add up to 1. CS2 case odds: Mil-Spec 79.92%, Restricted 15.98%, Classified 3.20%, Covert 0.64%, knife or gloves 0.26%. Add them: exactly 100%.
  • “Not A” is the rest: p(not A) = 1 − p(A). No knife from a case: 1 − 0.0026 = 0.9974.

How to read the symbols

  • p(A): the probability that A happens.
  • p(A, B): the probability that A and B both happen.
  • p(A | B): the probability of A given B. Read the bar “|” as “if we already know that”.
View 1

Frequentist

“p(knife) = 0.0026” means: open a huge number of cases and about 26 in every 10,000 give a knife. Probability is a long-run frequency.

View 2 · this course

Bayesian

“Team Spirit wins this TI final with probability 0.6” means: that is how sure I am. This final happens only once, so you cannot repeat it 1000 times. Probability measures uncertainty.

Part 3 · 7 min

Joint, marginal, conditional

These three words come up in every lecture. One table explains all of them. We looked at 100 CS2 rounds of one team and wrote down two things: did they get the opening kill (the first kill of the round), and did they win the round?

got opening killlost opening killtotal
won round301040
lost round55560
total3565100
One cell

Joint

Both things at once. Opening kill and round win:

p(win, open) = 30/100 = 0.30

One total

Marginal

Forget the other variable. Add up the column. This is the sum rule:

p(open) = 0.30 + 0.05 = 0.35

Zoom in, then count

Conditional

Look only at rounds with the opening kill (35 of them). How many were won?

p(win | open) = 30/35 ≈ 0.86

The two formulas behind it

p(A | B) = p(A, B)p(B)Conditional = joint ÷ marginal
p(A, B) = p(A | B) · p(B)The product rule. Check: 0.857 × 0.35 = 0.30 ✓

The classic beginner mistake

p(win | open) and p(open | win) are different numbers.

Win, given the opening kill: 30 out of 35 = 0.86.
Opening kill, given a win: 30 out of 40 = 0.75.

The order matters. What comes after the bar is the group you zoom into. Bayes' rule, next, is the tool that turns one into the other.

Part 4 · 8 min

Bayes' rule

Write the product rule two ways. Both describe the same joint probability, so they are equal:

p(A | B) · p(B) = p(A, B) = p(B | A) · p(A)

Divide both sides by p(B) and you have it:

p(A | B) = p(B | A) · p(A)p(B)

In the course, the letters change. θ (“theta”) is the unknown thing you want to learn, like a player's true headshot rate. D is the data you saw. Each piece has a name, and the exam will ask for them:

p(θ | D)

Posterior

Belief about θ after seeing the data.

p(D | θ)

Likelihood

How probable the data is if θ were true.

p(θ)

Prior

Belief about θ before the data.

p(D)

Evidence

Just a number that makes everything add up to 1.

The trick you will use on every exam

The evidence p(D) does not depend on θ. So we often drop it and write “∝”, which means “proportional to”:

p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) · p(θ)

Multiply the likelihood by the prior, look at the shape you get, and divide by the total at the very end.

Part 4 · example

Anti-cheat flagged a player. Are they really cheating?

Imagine an anti-cheat system (made-up numbers). 1% of players cheat. The system flags 90% of cheaters. It also wrongly flags 5% of clean players. Most people guess “90%, ban them”. Let's count instead.

Imagine 1000 players
10 cheaters90% get flagged → 9 flagged
The other 990
990 clean5% get flagged → about 50 flagged
15%

9 cheaters out of 59 flagged players. That is the real chance.

The same thing with Bayes' rule

p(cheat | flag) = 0.9 × 0.010.9 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0090.0585 ≈ 0.15

The bottom line is p(flag), found with the sum rule: flags on cheaters plus flags on clean players. The lesson: the prior matters a lot. When cheaters are rare, most flagged players are innocent. That is why a single flag is not enough for a ban.

Part 5 · 5 min

Random variables and distributions

Random variable

A number whose value depends on chance. Example: X = number of headshots in your next 10 kills. A distribution says how likely each value is.

Discrete (counts 0, 1, 2, …): p(x) is a probability, and all values add up to 1.

Continuous (any number, like your ping in ms): p(x) is a density. The area under the curve is the probability. The total area is 1.

Mean and variance

The mean or expected value E[X] is the long-run average. A Dota mid player gets about 60 last hits at 10 minutes on average, though single games go higher or lower.

The variance measures spread: how far values usually land from the mean. A consistent player has small variance. A player who goes 40 kills one game and 3 the next has big variance.

The distributions you will meet in this course:

one yes/no

Bernoulli(θ)

One yes/no. Is this one kill a headshot? p(1) = θ, p(0) = 1 − θ.

count of yes

Binomial(n, θ)

Headshots in n kills. With θ = 0.5 and 10 kills, the mean is nθ = 5.

wait for success

Geometric(q)

Cases opened before your first knife: p(k | q) = (1 − q)k q, q = 0.0026. On average about 384 cases. It was on both past finals.

K-sided die

Categorical / Multinomial

Like a coin with K sides. Which of 120+ Dota heroes will the enemy pick next? In the course: words in text.

tomorrow!

Beta(a, b)

A distribution for a probability itself, with values from 0 to 1. Your belief about a player's headshot rate. Mean a/(a+b).

the bell curve

Gaussian N(μ, σ²)

Center μ, width σ. Last hits at 10 minutes over many games. Comes back in classification and regression.

Part 6 · 2 min

Uncorrelated is not independent

Independent means knowing X tells you nothing about Y: p(x, y) = p(x) · p(y).

Correlation only measures a straight-line relationship.

Let X be how far your crosshair is from the enemy's head, sideways: left is negative, right is positive, anywhere from −1 to 1. Let Y = X² be how bad the miss is. Y depends on X completely. But missing left and missing right are equally bad, so the U-shape is symmetric, the two sides cancel, and the correlation is 0.

So “uncorrelated means independent” is false. This was a true/false question on the 2026 final. The other direction is true: independent always means uncorrelated.

Strong dependence, zero correlation.

Part 7 · 7 min

The Bayesian recipe, with headshots

A new player joins your team. Let p be their true headshot rate. Like in the lecture, pretend p can only be 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 or 1. Before any data, all five are equally likely: that is our prior.

Step 1 · data

H, B, H

Their first three kills: headshot, body shot, headshot.

Step 2 · likelihood

p × (1−p) × p

A headshot has probability p, a body shot 1−p. Multiply for each kill.

Step 3 · posterior

Multiply, then normalize

Likelihood × prior for each value, then divide by the total so it all adds up to 1.

Try it: add kills and watch the belief move

The value 0 dies after the first headshot. The value 1 dies after the first body shot. More kills make the bars sharper.

MLE

Maximum likelihood: the value that makes the data most probable. It ignores the prior. For H B H it is 0.75 among our five values. If every value from 0 to 1 is allowed, it is 2/3, the share of headshots.

MAP

Maximum a posteriori: the peak of the posterior. With a flat prior it equals the MLE. With a different prior it can move.

Posterior mean

The average over the posterior: 0.25·0.15 + 0.5·0.40 + 0.75·0.45 ≈ 0.58. It uses the whole belief, not only the peak.

Tomorrow, the same idea with all values

The Beta-Binomial model lets p be any number between 0 and 1. The prior becomes a Beta(a, b) curve. After k headshots in n kills, the posterior is again a Beta: Beta(a + k, b + n − k). You add the counts. That is the whole first chapter.

Part 8 · 4 min

Check yourself

Try each one on paper first, then open the answer.

1. From the rounds table: what is p(lost round | lost opening kill)?

Zoom into the “lost opening kill” column: 65 rounds. 55 of them were lost. So 55/65 ≈ 0.85.

2. Is p(open | win) the same as p(win | open)?

No. p(open | win) = 30/40 = 0.75 but p(win | open) = 30/35 ≈ 0.86. The group after the bar is different.

3. Same anti-cheat, but in a lobby where 10% of players cheat. p(cheat | flagged)?

1000 players: 100 cheaters → 90 flagged. 900 clean → 5% = 45 flagged. So 90 / (90 + 45) ≈ 0.67. The prior went up, so the posterior went up a lot.

4. True or false: if X and Y are uncorrelated, they are independent.

False. Counterexample: crosshair offset X uniform on (−1, 1) and miss size Y = X². They are uncorrelated, but Y is completely determined by X.

5. The new player goes H, H, H with the flat prior. What is the posterior?

Likelihood is p³: 0, 0.016, 0.125, 0.42, 1. The total is 1.5625. Divide: 0, 0.01, 0.08, 0.27, 0.64. Check it in the toy: press “Clear”, then “+ Headshot” three times. Note how sure the posterior gets about p = 1 after only 3 kills. Tomorrow's priors help with that.

Practice · 30–40 min

Practice problems

Exam-style tasks, now with Dota. Write the full solution on paper, then open the hint only if you are stuck, and the solution at the end.

1Easy

Carry picks and wins

You played 200 Dota games and wrote down two things:

wonlosttotal
you picked a carry6040100
other role4555100
total10595200

Find (a) p(win), (b) p(win | carry), (c) p(carry | win). (d) Are “carry” and “win” independent?

Hint

For a conditional, zoom into the group after the bar and count inside it. Independent means p(win | carry) = p(win).

Solution
  • (a) 105/200 = 0.525
  • (b) carry row: 60/100 = 0.60
  • (c) won column: 60/105 ≈ 0.571
  • (d) 0.60 ≠ 0.525, so they are not independent: picking a carry changes your win chance.
2Medium

Smurf detector

In your rank, 5% of accounts are smurfs. A detector flags 80% of smurfs, but it also flags 10% of normal accounts. (a) A player is flagged. What is p(smurf | flag)? (b) A second, independent detector with the same rates also flags them. What is the probability now?

Hint

Imagine 1000 accounts, or use Bayes' rule with the sum rule in the bottom line. For (b), both flags together have likelihood 0.8² for smurfs and 0.1² for normal accounts.

Solution
  • (a) 0.8·0.05 / (0.8·0.05 + 0.1·0.95) = 0.04 / 0.135 ≈ 0.30
  • (b) 0.64·0.05 / (0.64·0.05 + 0.01·0.95) = 0.032 / 0.0415 ≈ 0.77
  • Lesson: one flag is weak evidence when smurfs are rare. Two independent flags change the picture a lot.
3Medium

Bounty rune and the mid lane

You get the first bounty rune with probability 0.6. If you get it, you win the mid lane with probability 0.7; if not, with probability 0.4. (a) What is p(win lane)? (b) You won the lane. What is the probability that you had the rune?

Hint

(a) is the sum rule: split by “rune” and “no rune”. (b) is Bayes' rule with the answer of (a) in the bottom line.

Solution
  • (a) 0.7·0.6 + 0.4·0.4 = 0.42 + 0.16 = 0.58
  • (b) 0.42 / 0.58 ≈ 0.72
4Exam-style

Posterior on a grid with a real prior

A new Mirana player's arrow hit rate p is 0.25, 0.5 or 0.75, with prior 0.2, 0.5, 0.3. You see: hit, hit, miss. (a) Compute the posterior. (b) Give the MLE and the MAP among the three values. (c) What is the probability that the next arrow hits?

Hint

Likelihood is p²(1 − p). Multiply by the prior, then divide by the total. For (c), average p over the posterior.

Solution
  • Likelihood: 0.0469, 0.125, 0.1406. Times prior: 0.0094, 0.0625, 0.0422. Total 0.1141.
  • (a) Posterior: 0.08, 0.55, 0.37
  • (b) MLE = 0.75 (largest likelihood). MAP = 0.5 (largest posterior). The prior pulled the answer down.
  • (c) 0.25·0.08 + 0.5·0.55 + 0.75·0.37 ≈ 0.57. This is the posterior predictive.
5Exam-style

True or false, with a reason

(a) If p(A | B) = p(A), then also p(B | A) = p(B). (b) p(win | first blood) and p(first blood | win) are always equal. (c) The posterior is fully described by the two numbers MLE and MAP.

Hint

For (a), plug p(A | B) = p(A) into Bayes' rule for p(B | A).

Solution
  • (a) True. p(B | A) = p(A | B)p(B)/p(A) = p(A)p(B)/p(A) = p(B). Independence goes both ways.
  • (b) False. They are equal only if p(win) = p(first blood); in Part 3 the two directions gave 0.86 and 0.75.
  • (c) False. Two numbers cannot fix a whole distribution. This was a 2026 final question.
6Challenge

Watch the posterior move

Use the toy in Part 7 (it works for any yes/no data). Start from “Clear” each time. What posterior does p = 0.5 get after H B, after H H B B, and after H H H B B B? Explain what more data does to the bars.

Hint

All three have the same share of hits. Compare how tall the 0.5 bar is in each case.

Solution

All three have MLE 0.5, but the posterior for 0.5 grows: 0.40 → 0.47 → 0.54, while 0.25 and 0.75 shrink (check in the toy). The likelihood pk(1 − p)n−k gets sharper as n grows, so more data means more certainty, even with the same share.
