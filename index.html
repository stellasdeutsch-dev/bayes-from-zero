Part 1 · 3 min

What is statistical learning?

Statistical learning means using data to guess something you cannot see directly, and also saying how sure you are about that guess.

Some examples from your course: Is this email spam? What is the next word in a sentence? Will this student pass? How much of the Earth is covered by water?

Your course does this the Bayesian way. You start with a belief. You see some data. You update the belief. Coins, dice, spam filters, Gaussians and regression all repeat this one move. Once it clicks, the course becomes much easier.

The first lecture opened with a game. Throw an inflatable globe, catch it, and look at where your right index finger lands: Water or Land. Do it many times. From the results, guess the share of the Earth that is water. We will play this game ourselves in Part 7.

Before the data

Prior

What you believe before looking. “I think water is maybe half of the Earth.”

From the data

Likelihood

How well each possible answer explains what you saw. “If water were 90%, getting so much land would be strange.”

After the data

Posterior

Your updated belief. Prior and likelihood combined.

Part 2 · 4 min

Probability is a number for “how sure”

Three basic rules

  • A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • All possible outcomes together add up to 1. A fair die has 6 sides, so each side gets 1/6.
  • “Not A” is the rest: p(not A) = 1 − p(A). If rain is 0.3, no rain is 0.7.

How to read the symbols

  • p(A): the probability that A happens.
  • p(A, B): the probability that A and B both happen.
  • p(A | B): the probability of A given B. Read the bar “|” as “if we already know that”.
View 1

Frequentist

“p(heads) = 0.5” means: if you flip the coin very many times, about half the flips are heads. Probability is a long-run frequency.

View 2 · this course

Bayesian

“p(heads) = 0.5” means: I am equally unsure about heads and tails for the next flip. Probability measures uncertainty. It also works for things that happen only once, like one election.

Joint, marginal, conditional

These three words come up in every lecture. One table explains all of them. We looked at 100 emails and counted two things: is it spam, and does it contain the word “free”?

has “free”no “free”total
spam301040
not spam55560
total3565100
One cell

Joint

Both things at once. Spam and “free”:

p(spam, free) = 30/100 = 0.30

One total

Marginal

Forget the other variable. Add up the column. This is the sum rule:

p(free) = 0.30 + 0.05 = 0.35

Zoom in, then count

Conditional

Look only at emails with “free” (35 of them). How many are spam?

p(spam | free) = 30/35 ≈ 0.86

The two formulas behind it

p(A | B) = p(A, B)p(B)Conditional = joint ÷ marginal
p(A, B) = p(A | B) · p(B)The product rule. Check: 0.857 × 0.35 = 0.30 ✓

The classic beginner mistake

p(spam | free) and p(free | spam) are different numbers.

Spam given “free”: 30 out of 35 = 0.86.
“Free” given spam: 30 out of 40 = 0.75.

The order matters. What comes after the bar is the group you zoom into. Bayes' rule, next, is the tool that turns one into the other.

Part 4 · 8 min

Bayes' rule

Write the product rule two ways. Both describe the same joint probability, so they are equal:

p(A | B) · p(B) = p(A, B) = p(B | A) · p(A)

Divide both sides by p(B) and you have it:

p(A | B) = p(B | A) · p(A)p(B)

In the course, the letters change. θ (“theta”) is the unknown thing you want to learn, like the share of water. D is the data you saw. Each piece has a name, and the exam will ask for them:

p(θ | D)

Posterior

Belief about θ after seeing the data.

p(D | θ)

Likelihood

How probable the data is if θ were true.

p(θ)

Prior

Belief about θ before the data.

p(D)

Evidence

Just a number that makes everything add up to 1.

The trick you will use on every exam

The evidence p(D) does not depend on θ. So we often drop it and write “∝”, which means “proportional to”:

p(θ | D) ∝ p(D | θ) · p(θ)

Multiply the likelihood by the prior, look at the shape you get, and divide by the total at the very end.

Part 4 · example

You test positive. How worried should you be?

1% of people have a disease. The test catches 90% of sick people. It also wrongly says “positive” for 5% of healthy people. Most people guess 90%. Let's count instead.

The other 990

9 sick out of 59 positives. That is your real chance.

The same thing with Bayes' rule

p(sick | +) = 0.9 × 0.010.9 × 0.01 + 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0090.0585 ≈ 0.15

The bottom line is p(+), found with the sum rule: positives from sick people plus positives from healthy people. The lesson: the prior matters a lot. When the disease is rare, most positive tests are false alarms.

Part 5 · 5 min

Random variables and distributions

Random variable

A number whose value depends on chance. Example: X = number of heads in 10 flips. A distribution says how likely each value is.

Discrete (counts 0, 1, 2, …): p(x) is a probability, and all values add up to 1.

Continuous (any number, like height): p(x) is a density. The area under the curve is the probability. The total area is 1.

Mean and variance

The mean or expected value E[X] is the long-run average. Flip a fair coin 10 times, again and again: on average you get 5 heads.

The variance measures spread: how far values usually land from the mean. Small variance means values stay close to the mean.

The distributions you will meet in this course:

one yes/no

Bernoulli(θ)

One coin flip. p(1) = θ, p(0) = 1 − θ. Is this one email spam?

count of yes

Binomial(n, θ)

Number of heads in n flips. Mean nθ.

wait for success

Geometric(q)

Failures before the first success: p(k | q) = (1 − q)k q. It was on both past finals.

K-sided die

Categorical / Multinomial

Like a coin with K sides. Used for words in text and spam filters.

tomorrow!

Beta(a, b)

A distribution for a probability itself, with values from 0 to 1. Your prior for a coin. Mean a/(a+b).

the bell curve

Gaussian N(μ, σ²)

Center μ, width σ. Comes back in classification and regression.

Part 6 · 2 min

Uncorrelated is not independent

Independent means knowing X tells you nothing about Y: p(x, y) = p(x) · p(y).

Correlation only measures a straight-line relationship.

Take X uniform between −1 and 1, and Y = X². Y depends on X completely. But the U-shape is symmetric: X = −0.5 and X = 0.5 give the same Y, so the left and right sides cancel and the correlation is 0.

So “uncorrelated means independent” is false. This was a true/false question on the 2026 final. The other direction is true: independent always means uncorrelated.

Strong dependence, zero correlation.

Part 7 · 7 min

The Bayesian recipe, with the globe

Let p be the share of water. Like in the lecture, pretend p can only be 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 or 1. Before any data, all five are equally likely: that is our prior.

Step 1 · data

W, L, W

Three tosses: water, land, water.

Step 2 · likelihood

p × (1−p) × p

Water has probability p, land has 1−p. Multiply for each toss.

Step 3 · posterior

Multiply, then normalize

Likelihood × prior for each value, then divide by the total so it all adds up to 1.

Try it: add tosses and watch the belief move

The value 0 dies as soon as you see one W. The value 1 dies as soon as you see one L. More data makes the bars sharper.

MLE

Maximum likelihood: the value that makes the data most probable. It ignores the prior. For W L W it is 0.75 among our five values. If every value from 0 to 1 is allowed, it is 2/3, the share of W.

MAP

Maximum a posteriori: the peak of the posterior. With a flat prior it equals the MLE. With a different prior it can move.

Posterior mean

The average over the posterior: 0.25·0.15 + 0.5·0.40 + 0.75·0.45 ≈ 0.58. It uses the whole belief, not only the peak.

Tomorrow, the same idea with all values

The Beta-Binomial model lets p be any number between 0 and 1. The prior becomes a Beta(a, b) curve. After k waters in n tosses, the posterior is again a Beta: Beta(a + k, b + n − k). You add the counts. That is the whole first chapter.

Part 8 · 4 min

Check yourself

Try each one on paper first, then open the answer.

1. From the email table: what is p(not spam | no “free”)?

Zoom into the “no free” column: 65 emails. 55 of them are not spam. So 55/65 ≈ 0.85.

2. Is p(free | spam) the same as p(spam | free)?

No. p(free | spam) = 30/40 = 0.75 but p(spam | free) = 30/35 ≈ 0.86. The group after the bar is different.

3. Same test, but now 10% of people have the disease. p(sick | positive)?

1000 people: 100 sick → 90 positive. 900 healthy → 5% = 45 positive. So 90 / (90 + 45) ≈ 0.67. The prior went up, so the posterior went up a lot.

4. True or false: if X and Y are uncorrelated, they are independent.

False. Counterexample: X uniform on (−1, 1) and Y = X². They are uncorrelated, but Y is completely determined by X.

5. Globe data W, W, W with the flat prior. What is the posterior?

Likelihood is p³: 0, 0.016, 0.125, 0.42, 1. The total is 1.5625. Divide: 0, 0.01, 0.08, 0.27, 0.64. Click “+ Water” three times after a reset to check. Note how sure the posterior gets about p = 1 after only 3 tosses. Tomorrow's priors help with that.
